Would You Rather is a conversation game where players must choose between two different options. The choices can be funny, difficult, thoughtful or completely ridiculous.

You do not need a board, cards, score sheet or special equipment. All you need is at least two people and a collection of questions.

That simplicity makes it useful for parties, classrooms, family nights, road trips, dates and virtual gatherings.

## What you need to play

The basic game requires very little:

- At least two players
- A list of Would You Rather questions
- Enough time for everyone to answer
- A willingness to explain your choices

You can read questions from a prepared list, create your own or use our [interactive Would You Rather game](/).

For a quick round, five to ten questions may be enough. For a party or classroom activity, prepare a larger collection so you can adjust the questions to the group.

## The basic Would You Rather rules

The traditional version has three simple steps.

### 1. Read one question

One player reads a question containing two choices.

For example:

> Would you rather be able to fly or become invisible?

Both options should be understandable, and everyone should answer the same question.

### 2. Choose option A or option B

Every player must select one option. Players should not choose “both” or “neither” unless your group agrees to allow it before starting.

Requiring a choice is what makes the game interesting. Even when neither option is perfect, players must decide which one they prefer.

### 3. Explain the choice

After everyone answers, invite players to explain why they chose that option.

The explanation often creates more conversation than the question itself. One person may choose invisibility to explore unnoticed, while someone else may choose flying to avoid traffic.

There is normally no correct answer and no winner. The objective is to compare opinions, learn something about the other players and enjoy the discussion.

## How to play with a group

For a small group, players can answer one at a time. In a larger group, use a method that allows everyone to respond quickly.

### Raise your hand

Read the question and ask players to raise their hands for option A, followed by option B. This works well in classrooms and meetings.

### Move to different sides

Designate one side of the room as option A and the other as option B. Players physically move to the side representing their choice.

This version adds movement and makes the division of opinions immediately visible.

### Vote at the same time

Ask everyone to hold up one finger for option A and two fingers for option B. Count down from three so people reveal their choices simultaneously.

Simultaneous voting prevents players from simply following the most popular answer.

### Take turns asking questions

Let each player read the next question. Rotating the question reader keeps everyone involved and gives the host a break.

## Useful game variations

The standard rules are intentionally flexible. Choose a variation based on the people playing and the amount of time available.

### Quick-fire round

Set a timer and allow only five or ten seconds for each decision. Players answer immediately without overthinking.

After several quick questions, return to the answers that caused the biggest disagreement and discuss them.

### Guess someone else’s answer

Before a player reveals their choice, ask the group to predict what that person will choose.

This version works especially well among close friends, couples and family members who believe they know each other well.

### Defend the unpopular choice

If nearly everyone selects the same answer, ask someone from the smaller group to defend the alternative.

The purpose is not to prove anyone wrong. It encourages players to consider a different perspective and usually creates a lively conversation.

### Team discussion

Divide a larger group into small teams. Give each team a question and ask them to agree on one answer.

Afterward, each team explains how it reached its decision. This variation works well for classrooms, youth groups and team-building activities.

### Points version

Would You Rather does not require scoring, but you can add points if your group prefers competition.

Award one point for:

- Correctly predicting another player’s answer
- Giving the funniest explanation
- Persuading someone to change their choice
- Choosing the least popular option

Agree on the scoring rules before starting so the game remains fair and easy to follow.

## Choosing questions for your audience

A good question should fit the age, relationship and comfort level of the players.

### Children and families

Use imaginative, positive and easy-to-understand choices. Animals, superpowers, food, school and fictional worlds are usually safe subjects.

Browse our [Would You Rather categories](/categories/) to find questions suited to different groups.

### Friends and parties

Funny, embarrassing and unexpected choices can create memorable conversations. Avoid questions that target a particular person or make someone feel pressured to disclose private information.

### Couples

Use a mixture of playful preferences and thoughtful relationship questions. The objective should be curiosity—not testing whether a partner gives the “correct” answer.

### Classrooms

Choose age-appropriate questions that can encourage speaking, writing, reasoning or movement. Teachers can ask students to explain an answer in one sentence or write a short argument supporting their choice.

### Work groups

Keep the questions inclusive and appropriate for a professional setting. Travel, food, habits, imaginary abilities and everyday preferences are safer than personal relationships, money or controversial topics.

## What makes a good question?

The strongest Would You Rather questions usually have four qualities:

1. **Both choices are appealing or difficult.** If one answer is obviously better, the discussion ends quickly.
2. **The choices are easy to understand.** Players should not need a long explanation before answering.
3. **The question creates follow-up conversation.** A strong question makes people want to explain their reasoning.
4. **The subject fits the group.** A funny adults-only question may not be appropriate for children or coworkers.

Compare these examples:

- Easy choice: Would you rather receive a gift or lose your phone?
- Better choice: Would you rather receive the perfect gift today or plan the perfect vacation next year?

The second question creates a more balanced decision and reveals something about the player’s priorities.

## Common problems and simple fixes

### Everyone copies the first answer

Use simultaneous voting. Ask everyone to reveal their decision after a countdown.

### Players keep choosing neither

Remind the group that making a difficult choice is the main rule. If necessary, allow one “pass” per player during the entire game.

### The discussion takes too long

Use a timer or limit explanations to thirty seconds. Save the most interesting disagreements for the end.

### Someone feels uncomfortable

Skip the question immediately without asking the person to explain. A game should never require someone to discuss a subject they would rather avoid.

### The questions feel repetitive

Mix different styles:

- Funny questions
- Difficult choices
- Imaginative scenarios
- Everyday preferences
- Thoughtful conversation starters

Changing the tone keeps longer sessions interesting.

## Example of a complete round

Imagine four friends receive this question:

> Would you rather always arrive thirty minutes early or always arrive ten minutes late?

Two players select “early,” while two select “late.”

The early group explains that waiting is less stressful than rushing. The late group argues that arriving early wastes too much time.

The question takes only a few seconds to answer, but the explanations reveal different attitudes toward planning, time and social expectations. That conversation is the real purpose of the game.

## Final tips for a better game

Keep these principles in mind:

- Start with easy, funny questions
- Let everyone answer before beginning the discussion
- Mix light questions with more thoughtful choices
- Skip anything that makes a player uncomfortable
- Encourage explanations without forcing them
- End while the group is still enjoying the game

Would You Rather works because the rules are simple and the answers are personal. Choose questions that fit your group, give everyone space to explain their reasoning and avoid taking the decisions too seriously.
